# Space to Grow: Unlocking the Final Economic Frontier
## Chapter 1: The Inflection Point: Crisis into Opportunity

### shuttle-gap (importance 3)

The shuttle was America's only means of putting humans in space, so its retirement left the US with no homegrown way to reach the ISS and forced it to buy seats on Russia's Soyuz.

> The shuttle was America's only means of putting humans in space, so its cancellation made apparent to everyone the lack of any homegrown alternatives for launching crew and cargo to the ISS

**Q1. [recall]** When the shuttle program ended, how did the United States plan to get its astronauts to the ISS?

- ✅ By paying Russia for seats on Soyuz
- By flying them on SpaceX's Dragon capsule
- By using the Constellation program's crew rocket
- By suspending US crew flights until a replacement existed

_No domestic alternative existed in 2011. Constellation had been canceled and commercial crew did not fly until 2020, so the only option was buying Soyuz seats for tens of millions of dollars each._

**Q2. [comprehension]** Why do the authors treat the shuttle's retirement as an opportunity and not only a crisis?

- ✅ It left a void that made room to change how NASA worked with industry
- It freed up enough budget to fully fund a return to the Moon
- It ended the political pressure to protect jobs in key districts
- It forced Congress to restore NASA's Apollo-era share of spending

_The shuttle's dominance had starved alternatives of funding and missions. Its absence was the blank slate that let NASA look to the commercial sector. The political pressures and budget limits did not go away._

### public-goods-rationale (importance 3)

The early space program was centrally run because its priorities (national prestige, national security, science, exploration) are public goods, which markets underprovide and governments do best.

> the priorities of the early space program—national prestige, national security, science, and exploration—are classic examples of activities that governments do best, what economists call public goods

**Q3. [comprehension]** According to the authors, why was central government control a natural fit for the early US space program?

- ✅ Its goals were public goods that markets would underprovide
- Private firms lacked the engineering talent to build rockets
- Space law at the time barred companies from launching
- Congress distrusted contractors after wartime procurement scandals

_Prestige, security, science and exploration are public goods. Private contractors did build the hardware, but public officials set the goals, funding, timelines and requirements._

**Q4. [application, reasoning]** By the chapter's logic, which of these space activities would be the weakest candidate for handing to the market?

- ✅ A basic-science probe to study the outer planets
- Routine cargo delivery to a space station
- Launching commercial communications satellites
- Suborbital tourism flights

_Basic research is a public good with no paying customer, so the market would underprovide it. The other three are routine services with identifiable buyers, where competition and prices can work._

### central-control-weaknesses (importance 3)

Central control has three structural weaknesses: little competition means weak incentives for efficiency and innovation, price signals deteriorate so resources can't be allocated well, and officials face pressure to serve concentrated interests.

> The lack of the competition that you see in a free market means there are limited incentives for efficiency and innovation. Over time, price signals deteriorate, so determining the best way to allocate resources becomes nearly impossible

**Q5. [recall]** Which of these is NOT one of the structural weaknesses of central control the authors identify?

- ✅ Planners lack the technical expertise to judge engineering work
- Weak incentives for efficiency and innovation without competition
- Price signals that deteriorate until allocation becomes guesswork
- Pressure on officials to serve concentrated interests

_The authors stress that the problem was structural and 'wasn't really anyone's fault'. NASA's engineers were world-class. The three weaknesses are missing competition, missing prices and capture by concentrated interests._

**Q6. [comprehension]** The authors say that under central control 'price signals deteriorate'. What is the practical consequence?

- ✅ Even well-meaning planners can't tell where resources are best used
- Contractors raise their prices faster than inflation
- The government loses the ability to audit what it has spent
- Private investors stop funding companies in the sector

_Prices carry information about true costs and benefits. Without them, allocating resources well becomes nearly impossible regardless of the planners' intentions._

### narrow-goal-vs-frontier (importance 3)

Centralized models perform well against a clear, narrow, fixed goal (Apollo, early Soviet industrialization) and struggle once the task becomes open-ended. The US space program kept its Apollo-era structure after the goal changed.

> America's initial moonshot program of the 1960s had a clear and narrow objective that was well suited to central control. Though the space program accomplished wonders after Apollo, its structure failed to evolve

**Q7. [comprehension]** What parallel do the authors draw between the Soviet economy and the US space program?

- ✅ Both thrived on narrow goals and faltered on open-ended ones
- Both collapsed once military spending crowded out civilian work
- Both depended on technology taken from a rival power
- Both failed because leaders ignored their own engineers

_The Soviet economy looked invincible while it had fixed short-term industrial goals, and Apollo had a clear narrow objective. Both centralized models struggled once they faced an uncertain frontier._

**Q8. [recall]** Space executive Jim Cantrell said the aerospace industry that beat the Soviets 'in the defense of capitalism' was itself operating on what?

- ✅ A Soviet economic model
- A wartime mobilization model
- A regulated-utility model
- A venture capital model

_Cantrell's irony: the industry that won the space race was centrally planned and isolated from market forces._

### space-race-security (importance 2)

From the start, US officials treated space as a national security matter, including the psychological effect of Soviet 'firsts' on national prestige. NASA was created in response to Sputnik (1957) and Gagarin's flight (1961).

> The Soviet Union's success in achieving numerous space "firsts," the document noted, "have resulted in substantial and enduring gains in Soviet prestige," bolstering claims of the Soviet system's "general superiority."

**Q9. [comprehension]** Why did Soviet space 'firsts' alarm US officials in the late 1950s?

- ✅ They bolstered claims that the Soviet system was superior
- They let the Soviets claim legal ownership of orbital slots
- They threatened the US commercial satellite industry
- They revealed that US rockets could not reach orbit at all

_A 1959 policy statement framed space in terms of national security and the 'psychological impact' on prestige. No commercial satellite industry existed yet._

### nasa-budget-share (importance 2)

NASA's budget peaked in 1966 at 4.4% of federal spending, fell to 1.2% by 1974, and has been around 0.5% for the past decade.

> At its high-water mark in 1966, the agency received $6 billion, or 4.4 percent of the federal budget.

**Q10. [recall]** At its 1966 peak, roughly what share of the federal budget went to NASA?

### apollo-public-opinion (importance 2)

Public support for space spending was weak even during Apollo: a year after the Moon landing 56% of Americans said it had not been worth the money. Support fell as the perceived Soviet threat faded.

> one year after the Moon landing (which inspired so many at the time, and so many of today's space leaders), 56 percent felt that it had not been worth the money

**Q11. [recall]** One year after Apollo 11, how did most Americans feel about what the Moon landing had cost?

- ✅ A majority said it had not been worth the money
- A majority said it was worth it and wanted a Mars mission next
- Opinion was evenly split along party lines
- A majority said it was worth it but wanted spending frozen

_56% said it had not been worth the money, even though 81% agreed nothing could equal watching it live. Admiration did not translate into willingness to pay._

**Q12. [comprehension]** What do the authors connect the decline in support for space funding to?

- ✅ The fading of the perceived Soviet threat from space
- The loss of the Apollo 1 crew in a launchpad fire
- The rising cost of the space shuttle program
- Scientists arguing that robotic probes were enough

_Funding followed the security rationale. Once the Soviet threat looked smaller, so did the case for the spending._

### act-one-vs-act-two (importance 3)

NASA's Act One (Apollo) was a rally-the-nation feat, which governments do well. Act Two (shuttle and ISS) required continuous improvement, efficiency and cost reduction, which large government programs do poorly.

> Act Two required the dogged pursuit of continuous improvement, innovation, efficiency, and cost reduction, not typically strengths we associate with large government programs.

**Q13. [comprehension]** What is the fundamental difference the authors draw between NASA's Act One and Act Two?

- ✅ Act One was a one-off feat. Act Two needed steady cost reduction
- Act One was crewed. Act Two relied mainly on robotic missions
- Act One was military-led. Act Two was run by civilians
- Act One was built in-house. Act Two was built by contractors

_Rallying a country around a seemingly impossible goal plays to a government's strengths. Making access routine and affordable requires continuous efficiency gains, which it does not._

**Q14. [application, reasoning]** The authors cite Operation Warp Speed as a modern 'Act One'. Which task would be the closest analogue to an 'Act Two'?

- ✅ Cutting vaccine production costs year after year
- Developing a vaccine for a newly emerged virus within a year
- Building the first working fusion power plant
- Landing the first humans on Mars

_Act Two work is ongoing, incremental cost and efficiency improvement. The other three are single, clearly defined feats that reward concentrated national effort._

### reusability-economics (importance 3)

The shuttle's economic case was reusability: a reusable vehicle costs more up front, but if it flies often, the high fixed cost is spread over many launches and the average cost per launch falls.

> if one vehicle could be used over and over, the high initial costs would be worth it because the average cost per launch could fall

**Q15. [comprehension]** The shuttle cost far more to develop than an expendable rocket. What had to be true for it to be cheaper per launch anyway?

- ✅ It had to fly often enough to spread its fixed costs
- It had to carry heavier payloads than any expendable rocket
- It had to be sold to commercial operators after development
- It had to replace its solid boosters with reusable ones

_Reusability only pays off through flight rate. The shuttle was projected to fly sixty missions a year and averaged fewer than five, so the average cost never came down._

**Q16. [application, reasoning]** A startup claims its fully reusable rocket will halve launch prices. Based on the shuttle's history, which question most directly tests that claim?

- ✅ How often will each vehicle fly per year?
- How much did the vehicle cost to develop?
- How many engines does the first stage use?
- What is the maximum payload it can lift to orbit?

_The shuttle hit its development cost target but missed on flight rate and operating cost by a wide margin. Flight rate decides whether reuse lowers average cost._

### shuttle-monopoly (importance 3)

To give the shuttle enough flights to spread its fixed costs, the government routed all defense payloads to it. This handed it a de facto monopoly, and US private expendable launchers were phased out by the late 1970s.

> The shuttle program was handed a de facto monopoly before it even started, and by the late 1970s US private launch vehicles were phased out.

**Q17. [comprehension]** Why were national security agencies told that the shuttle would carry all defense payloads?

- ✅ To spread its fixed costs over more missions
- Because expendable rockets had proven too unreliable for them
- Because only the shuttle could reach the orbits they required
- To keep classified hardware out of private contractors' hands

_The shuttle's economics depended on volume, and its political support depended on preserving Apollo-era jobs. Guaranteeing it the defense market served both._

**Q18. [recall]** What happened to US private expendable launch vehicles as the shuttle program took shape in the late 1970s?

- ✅ They were phased out
- They were limited to commercial satellites only
- They were merged into a single government-owned firm
- They were kept as a backup fleet for defense payloads

_The shuttle displaced an existing industry. This is why the Challenger-era and later efforts to revive commercial launch had to start nearly from scratch._

### shuttle-shortfall (importance 3)

The shuttle missed its economic goals badly: projected at sixty flights a year, it averaged fewer than five. Each launch cost about $1.5 billion, roughly $60,000 per kilogram, and the program totaled over $200 billion.

> Rather than flying sixty missions per year, as projected, the shuttles were flying fewer than ten; across their thirty years in operation, the shuttles would average fewer than five launches per year.

**Q19. [recall]** The shuttle was projected to fly sixty missions a year. What did it average over its thirty years?

- ✅ Fewer than five
- About twelve
- About twenty-five
- About forty

_Fewer than five a year, less than a tenth of the projection. That shortfall is what destroyed the cost case for reuse._

**Q20. [recall]** Roughly what did it cost the shuttle to put one kilogram of cargo in orbit?

- ✅ About $60,000
- About $2,800
- About $10,000
- About $500,000

_About $60,000 per kilogram (2008 dollars), from roughly $1.5 billion per launch. $2,800 is the Falcon 9 figure the chapter contrasts it with._

**Q21. [comprehension]** NASA hit its target for the shuttle's development cost. Where did the cost estimates go wrong?

- ✅ Operating costs came in nearly a hundred times higher
- The orbiters wore out and had to be rebuilt every few years
- Fuel prices rose far faster than planners had assumed
- Building the launch sites cost several times the estimate

_Scott Pace's assessment: development came in on target, but the cost of operating the vehicles safely was almost a hundred times higher than expected._

### iss-cost-critique (importance 2)

The ISS cost roughly $100 billion to build and $4 billion a year to maintain. Critics said the scientific returns were not commensurate, and the shuttle and ISS together cannibalized NASA's other programs.

> the station cost roughly $100 billion to create and $4 billion a year to maintain, and critics alleged that the scientific returns were "in no way commensurate."

**Q22. [recall]** What was the main criticism leveled at the ISS?

- ✅ Its scientific returns did not justify its cost
- Partnering with Russia gave away sensitive US technology
- It was too small to host meaningful experiments
- Its low orbit made it useless for deep-space research

_About $100 billion to build and $4 billion a year to run. The authors credit it as a diplomatic triumph, but critics found the science return far too small for the price._

### self-licking-ice-cream-cone (importance 3)

Pete Worden's 1992 'self-licking ice cream cone' critique: the shuttle and station justified each other (the station could only be built by the shuttle, and the shuttle's main job was building the station), sustaining jobs in key districts and leaving no money to develop anything cheaper.

> the Station is designed so that it can only be built by the Shuttle and the Shuttle is the only way to construct the Station

**Q23. [comprehension]** What did Pete Worden mean by calling the shuttle and space station a 'self-licking ice cream cone'?

- ✅ Each program existed mainly to justify the other
- Both produced plenty of spectacle but very little science
- Their budgets kept growing while their capabilities shrank
- Contractors were being paid twice for the same hardware

_The station could only be built by the shuttle, and building the station was the shuttle's purpose. The loop absorbed most of NASA's budget and created political pressure against escaping it._

**Q24. [application, reasoning]** Which situation best fits Worden's 'self-licking ice cream cone' pattern?

- ✅ A team that mainly produces reports on its own process
- A product that is profitable but losing market share
- A project that runs over budget because of bad estimates
- A startup that pivots each time its funding runs low

_The pattern is circular justification: an activity whose purpose is to sustain itself. Cost overruns and declining share are different failures._

### early-commercialization-blocked (importance 2)

From the Reagan era on, policy endorsed commercial launch (Delta became the first US-licensed commercial vehicle in 1989; the Commercial Space Act passed in 1998), but these efforts stayed marginal because the subsidized, government-run shuttle took the government's missions and most of the funding.

> Government efforts to spark new efficiency and innovation were being squashed by the primacy the government gave to its own vehicles.

**Q25. [comprehension]** Policies endorsing commercial launch date from the 1980s. Why did they have so little effect before the 2000s?

- ✅ The subsidized shuttle kept the government's missions
- No private company managed to build a working orbital rocket
- Insurers refused to cover commercially launched satellites
- The laws only allowed commercial launches from foreign sites

_Private rockets did fly, starting with Delta in 1989. As Wayne Eleazer put it, nothing stopped competitors except 'a well-funded, heavily-subsidized, government-run competitor'._

### aldridge-commission (importance 3)

After Columbia, the Aldridge Commission (2004) said NASA must be transformed: buy services from competing private firms instead of owning and operating everything, and limit its own role to what only government can do. This became law in the Commercial Space Launch Amendments Act of 2004.

> NASA's role "must be limited to only those areas where there is irrefutable demonstration that only government can perform the proposed activity."

**Q26. [recall]** What limit did the Aldridge Commission say should apply to NASA's own role?

- ✅ Only activities that demonstrably only government can perform
- Only crewed missions, leaving robotic science to industry
- Only missions beyond Earth orbit, leaving low orbit to industry
- Only research, leaving all hardware development to industry

_The test was whether there is 'irrefutable demonstration that only government can perform the proposed activity'. It is a principle, not a fixed list of mission types._

**Q27. [comprehension]** The Aldridge Commission considered recommending that some NASA centers be closed, then dropped it. Why?

- ✅ It would have been politically fatal to the whole report
- Analysis showed the centers were already running efficiently
- The centers were needed to build the Constellation rockets
- Closing them required a constitutional change to NASA's charter

_Chairman Edward Aldridge said the report 'would probably have burned on the first day'. It illustrates the concentrated-interest pressure the chapter describes._

### constellation-vs-cots (importance 3)

Two approaches to the post-shuttle gap ran in parallel. Constellation was the primary plan: a large, government-owned, Apollo-style program ('Apollo on steroids'). COTS was a small ($500 million) commercial experiment to have private companies develop ISS cargo vehicles.

> The primary plan was called Constellation, an expansive (and expensive) new program in the Apollo model.

**Q28. [recall]** NASA Administrator Michael Griffin described which program as 'Apollo on steroids'?

- ✅ Constellation
- COTS
- Commercial Crew
- The Vision for Space Exploration

_Constellation was the government-owned program with a Saturn V-class rocket, a lunar lander and a crew rocket. The Vision for Space Exploration was the policy goal it was meant to serve._

**Q29. [comprehension]** How did COTS differ from Constellation in who would own the vehicles?

- ✅ COTS vehicles were company-owned. Constellation's were not
- Both were government-owned, but COTS used smaller contractors
- Both were company-owned, but only Constellation carried crew
- COTS vehicles were jointly owned by NASA and its partners

_Every Constellation asset would be owned and operated by the government. Under COTS, companies designed and owned their vehicles and NASA bought a service._

**Q30. [recall]** What was the specific job the COTS program asked private companies to do?

- ✅ Develop vehicles to carry cargo to the ISS
- Carry NASA astronauts to the ISS
- Build a replacement for the space shuttle orbiter
- Launch NASA's science satellites at lower cost

_COTS was deliberately narrow: cargo to the ISS. Crew came later, under the Commercial Crew program that COTS's success made possible._

### far-cost-plus (importance 3)

Under traditional FAR contracting, NASA paid contractors cost-plus: their total costs plus a guaranteed profit margin. It existed to insulate companies from the technical and policy risk of ambitious space projects, and about 90% of space program work was contracted this way.

> By guaranteeing that private companies' costs would be covered, plus a margin, FAR made it possible for NASA to contract out much of its equipment needs.

**Q31. [recall]** Under a cost-plus contract, what does the government pay the contractor?

- ✅ All its costs, plus an additional amount for profit
- A fee agreed in advance, whatever the work ends up costing
- Its costs up to a cap, with overruns shared equally
- A base fee plus bonuses for hitting schedule milestones

_Cost-plus reimburses total cost and adds a margin. The first distractor describes fixed-price, the model COTS switched to._

**Q32. [comprehension]** Cost-plus contracting 'was not an accident'. What problem was it designed to solve?

- ✅ Shielding firms from the risk of uncertain projects
- Stopping contractors from underbidding to win work
- Giving small new companies a way into the industry
- Making sure the government owned what it paid for

_Space hardware needed huge up-front investment with technical and policy uncertainty. Guaranteeing costs made firms willing to take the work. It was hard on small entrants, which is one of the criticisms._

### far-criticisms (importance 3)

Criticisms of FAR cost-plus contracting: guaranteed margins gave firms weak incentives to control cost, heavy oversight and documentation burdened new entrants most, and the government took ownership of the technology and intellectual property.

> under FAR contracts, firms had weak incentives to keep costs down and pursue efficiencies, since their margins were guaranteed

**Q33. [comprehension]** Why did cost-plus contracts give firms weak incentives to keep costs down?

- ✅ Their margin was guaranteed on top of whatever they spent
- They were barred from keeping any savings they found
- NASA's specifications left no room to change the design
- Their contracts were renewed automatically each year

_If every dollar of cost is reimbursed with a margin on top, spending less earns the firm nothing._

**Q34. [comprehension]** Why did FAR contracting weigh hardest on young, small space companies?

- ✅ They lacked the staff to handle its paperwork
- They were legally excluded from bidding on FAR contracts
- They were paid lower margins than established contractors
- They had to post large bonds before starting any work

_FAR brought heavy requirements, documentation and supervision. Incumbents had the back-office infrastructure to absorb that, and new entrants did not._

### fixed-price (importance 3)

COTS used fixed-price contracts: NASA pays a set fee for a service. Financial risk shifts to the company, and the company profits by keeping its costs below the fee, which brings in the efficiency incentives of commercial markets.

> It puts the financial risk on the private companies, but the more that companies can keep their costs below that fee, the more they profit.

**Q35. [comprehension]** Compared with cost-plus, what does a fixed-price contract change about who bears financial risk?

- ✅ The company bears overruns and keeps any savings
- The government bears overruns but the company keeps savings
- Overruns and savings are split between the two
- The government bears overruns and recovers any savings

_A set fee means the company absorbs cost overruns and profits from efficiency. The last distractor is roughly how cost-plus works._

**Q36. [application, reasoning]** A company on a fixed-price contract finds a cheaper way to build its vehicle. What happens to the savings?

- ✅ The company keeps them as profit
- NASA's payment is reduced by the amount saved
- They are split according to a preset formula
- They must be reinvested in the same program

_The fee is fixed, so every dollar saved is margin. That is the incentive the model was chosen to create._

### insight-not-oversight (importance 3)

Under COTS, NASA offered 'insight rather than oversight'. Heavy oversight was necessary under cost-plus because firms had an incentive to inflate costs. Fixed-price removed that incentive, so NASA could give companies design freedom while still sharing its expertise.

> oversight was imperative in a cost-plus approach where companies had every incentive to inflate costs

**Q37. [comprehension]** Why was NASA able to relax its oversight of companies under COTS?

- ✅ Fixed-price removed the incentive to inflate costs
- Cargo missions carried no risk to human life
- Congress had cut the staff who performed oversight
- The COTS companies had proven track records with NASA

_Oversight guards against cost padding. Once the fee is fixed, padding only hurts the company, so the main reason for intrusive oversight disappears. The COTS winners were young, unproven firms._

**Q38. [recall]** Under COTS, what did NASA say it wanted to provide its partners in place of oversight?

- ✅ Insight: its engineers' expertise and experience
- Indemnity: legal cover if a launch failed
- Infrastructure: free use of its launch sites
- Investment: an equity stake in each company

_'Insight rather than oversight'. NASA's scientists and engineers shared what they knew without dictating the design._

### one-customer-among-many (importance 3)

Under COTS, NASA aimed to be 'one customer among many'. The clearest signal was letting companies keep the intellectual property for what they developed, so they could sell the same vehicles to other customers.

> NASA explicitly sought to be just "one customer among many" for its COTS partners, a goal made manifest by its allowing companies to keep the intellectual property rights to their new technologies.

**Q39. [recall]** Which COTS feature most clearly showed that NASA wanted to be 'one customer among many'?

- ✅ Companies kept the intellectual property they developed
- NASA capped its purchases at half of each company's launches
- Companies were required to sign a second customer first
- NASA paid only after each delivery was completed

_Under FAR the government owned the technology. Letting companies keep their IP meant they could build a business beyond NASA._

**Q40. [comprehension, reasoning]** Why did it matter to NASA that its COTS partners could find other customers?

- ✅ A wider market spreads costs and sustains the providers
- It let NASA collect royalties on each commercial launch
- It was a legal condition of using Space Act Agreements
- It let NASA walk away once the ISS was complete

_The stated goal of COTS was to 'open new space markets' while meeting ISS needs. Providers with other revenue are healthier and cheaper for NASA to buy from. NASA took no royalties._

### space-act-agreements (importance 1)

COTS avoided the FAR process by using Space Act Agreements, based on the 'other transactions' authority in NASA's founding act of 1958.

> it relied on NASA's authority to initiate "other transactions," granted to it in its founding act of 1958, to avoid using the FAR process and enter instead into much less burdensome Space Act Agreements

**Q41. [recall]** What legal instrument let COTS sidestep the standard FAR contracting process?

- ✅ Space Act Agreements
- The Commercial Space Act of 1998
- A presidential executive order
- A waiver granted by the Aldridge Commission

_NASA used the 'other transactions' authority it has had since its founding act of 1958. No new legislation was needed._

### kistler-termination (importance 2)

The first COTS awards (2006) went to SpaceX and Rocketplane Kistler. When Kistler failed to raise private capital and missed milestones, NASA terminated its contract and Orbital Sciences took it over, showing NASA would act as a real customer.

> It began missing milestones, and NASA terminated its contract. The agency, it appeared, was taking its new role as a customer seriously.

**Q42. [comprehension]** What did NASA's handling of Rocketplane Kistler demonstrate about the COTS approach?

- ✅ NASA would drop a partner that missed its milestones
- NASA would add funding to rescue a struggling partner
- Small companies could not survive on fixed-price contracts
- NASA preferred established contractors after all

_Kistler could not raise enough private capital, missed milestones and was terminated. Under the old model a failing program would more likely have been given more money._

**Q43. [recall]** Roughly how much of SpaceX's costs during COTS did NASA's funding cover?

- ✅ Slightly less than half
- About a tenth
- About three quarters
- Nearly all of it

_NASA put in nearly $400 million and SpaceX raised just over $450 million privately. Companies were expected to bring their own capital, which is what Kistler failed to do._

### constellation-cancelled (importance 2)

Constellation ran over cost and behind schedule and was canceled under Obama in 2010, with NASA deputy administrator Lori Garver the leading advocate for the commercial path. Opponents argued that trusting companies with cargo was optimistic and with crew irresponsible, and that tens of thousands of jobs were at stake.

> relying on the private sector to bring cargo to the ISS would be overly optimistic; relying on it (some day in the future) to deliver crew, NASA's most precious cargo, would be irresponsible

**Q44. [comprehension]** What was the main argument made against canceling Constellation in favor of commercial providers?

- ✅ If NASA couldn't make access cheap and safe, companies were unlikely to
- Commercial rockets would cost more per launch than Constellation
- Private firms would refuse to share their designs with NASA
- Foreign companies would end up winning the contracts

_Skeptics pointed to NASA's decades of experience without a cheap, safe, reliable route to orbit and asked what the odds were that a company could do better. Jobs at NASA and its contractors were also at stake._

**Q45. [comprehension]** How did Lori Garver argue that commercialization would benefit NASA itself?

- ✅ Companies doing routine work would free NASA for harder problems
- NASA could earn revenue by licensing its technology
- NASA could shrink until it was only a regulator
- Competition from companies would push NASA's own rockets to improve

_Garver's case was that NASA should stop competing with the private sector on routine tasks and invest in work of greater relevance to taxpayers, such as climate research._

### cots-results (importance 3)

COTS cost NASA about $850 million, half the cost of a single shuttle launch, and produced two new orbital rockets and cargo vehicles (SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Dragon, Orbital's Antares and Cygnus). It led to Commercial Resupply Services contracts and seeded Commercial Crew.

> At a cost to NASA of $850 million—half the cost of a single shuttle launch—the agency had helped develop two new orbital rockets and cargo vehicles.

**Q46. [recall]** What did NASA get for the roughly $850 million it spent on COTS?

- ✅ Two new orbital rockets and two cargo vehicles
- One rocket and a prototype crew capsule
- Design studies and prototypes, but no orbital flights
- Twenty cargo deliveries to the ISS

_Falcon 9 with Dragon and Antares with Cygnus. The routine deliveries were bought separately under the larger Commercial Resupply Services contracts._

**Q47. [recall]** The authors compare the total cost of COTS to which benchmark?

- ✅ Half the cost of a single shuttle launch
- One year of operating the ISS
- The price of a single Soyuz seat
- The entire development cost of the shuttle

_$850 million against roughly $1.5 billion per shuttle launch._

**Q48. [comprehension]** Why did NASA keep two cargo providers under Commercial Resupply Services when one could have done the job?

- ✅ As a hedge in case the new approach stumbled
- The law required every contract to have two suppliers
- Neither company could launch often enough alone
- Each company served a different part of the station

_Two providers hedged the risk of relying on a new model. The same logic keeps competition alive between suppliers._

### cots-commercial-legacy (importance 3)

COTS counted as an 'inflection point' because SpaceX began launching commercial satellites on the rockets developed under NASA contract. The benefit extended beyond the ISS, and NASA really was one customer among many.

> SpaceX was beginning to launch commercial satellites with the rockets developed under contract with NASA.

**Q49. [comprehension]** Beyond delivering cargo to the ISS, what made COTS an 'unqualified success' in the authors' telling?

- ✅ Its rockets went on to launch commercial satellites
- It finished ahead of its original schedule
- It persuaded Congress to raise NASA's budget
- Its rockets replaced Soyuz for crew flights within two years

_The point of COTS was to open markets. Once SpaceX sold launches to commercial customers, NASA's investment had created an industry capability and not just a NASA supplier. The program took seven years, longer than hoped._

### first-welfare-theorem-space (importance 3)

The chapter's economic frame is the First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics: markets are efficient when certain conditions hold. Public goods like security, prestige and basic science violate those conditions, which justified central control. As space ambitions shifted to activities where the conditions come closer to holding (such as routine transport), markets became the more efficient tool.

> as our ambitions for space grew, we sought objectives for which the "certain conditions" of the 1FTWE come closer to holding; that is, for which markets would be more efficient

**Q57. [comprehension]** In the authors' economic framing, what changed between the 1960s and the 2000s that made markets the better tool for space?

- ✅ The goals shifted toward activities where market conditions nearly hold
- Economists overturned the theory that had justified central control
- Technology matured until rockets were no longer risky to build
- Public goods like national security stopped mattering in space

_The theory did not change. Central control fit public goods, where the theorem's conditions fail. Routine transport to orbit is closer to an ordinary tradable service. Public goods still matter, and NASA keeps those._

**Q58. [comprehension]** According to the closing section, what did COTS-style policies leave NASA free to concentrate on?

- ✅ Exploration, prestige, international cooperation and basic research
- Regulating the safety of commercial launch providers
- Designing the rockets that companies would then operate
- Running a government fleet to compete with private launchers

_The division of labor is the point: markets for what markets do well, and NASA as central actor for goals 'less suited to private actors'._

**Q59. [application, reasoning]** By the chapter's framework, which condition would most justify keeping a space activity under central government control?

- ✅ Everyone benefits and no one can be charged for it
- It needs very large amounts of up-front capital
- It carries a high risk of technical failure
- It has never been attempted by a private company

_That is the definition of a public good, where the theorem's conditions fail. High capital needs and technical risk are things markets can handle, as SpaceX's privately raised funding showed._

### policy-mechanisms-summary (importance 3)

COTS and its follow-ons worked through four mechanisms: competition between private actors, prices reintroduced via fixed-price contracts, incentives for responsible risk-taking, and letting efficient companies keep the rewards.

> Carefully crafted policies like COTS and its follow-ons introduced competition between private actors, reintroduced prices through fixed-price contracts, incentivized responsible risk-taking, and allowed innovative and efficient companies to reap the rewards.

**Q60. [comprehension, reasoning]** Central control suffered from missing price signals. Which COTS feature addressed that weakness most directly?

- Insight in place of oversight
- Companies keeping their intellectual property
- Use of Space Act Agreements

_The authors say COTS 'reintroduced prices through fixed-price contracts'. A set fee for a service is a price, and it reveals who can deliver for less._

**Q61. [application, reasoning]** A space agency wants cheaper lunar cargo delivery. Which design is closest to the COTS model?

- ✅ Pay several competing firms a set fee per milestone and let them own the landers
- Pick the best firm, reimburse its costs and supervise its design closely
- Have agency engineers design the lander and firms bid to build it
- Fund one national champion and guarantee it all agency missions

_COTS combined competition, fixed-price milestone payments and company ownership. The distractors are cost-plus with oversight, the traditional government-design model, and the shuttle's guaranteed monopoly._
